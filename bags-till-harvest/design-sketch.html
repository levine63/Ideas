Sketch · harvest-allocation exercise as a phone app

Bags Till Harvest

A clickable sketch of the Augenblick, Jack, Kaur, Masiye & Swanson planning exercise, rebuilt for low-literacy farmers and families. Tap through the phone. The panel beside it shows, for each screen, what the original script did and what this version changes. It opens with an example family already filled in; tap New plan on the first screen to start empty.

What the study did

Zambian maize farmers live on one harvest a year, and most households run out before the next one. In the Augenblick, Jack, Kaur, Masiye & Swanson field experiment, people underestimated their upcoming spending by about half. Treated households spent about an hour with a trained surveyor at harvest time, going through six expense categories one at a time (food, education, groceries and household goods, farm inputs, transfers, emergencies). They set pins standing for bags of grain into category boxes. The surveyor gave no advice and no outside information. Remembered expenses went up 42%, mostly small, irregular and uncertain items. Two months later savings were 15% higher, and households went into the hungry season with roughly one extra month of savings. The food-insecurity index moved in the right direction but was not statistically significant.

Rules this sketch keeps from the study

  • Recall, not advice. The app never says how much anything costs or what to cut. Every number comes from the family. That is what the trial tested. A version that adds price lists or advice is a different treatment and needs its own study arm.
  • One category at a time, with picture cues. The memory mechanism depends on the cues, not on the arithmetic.
  • Bags are the unit. Food, school fees and fertiliser are all counted in the same sacks, just like the pins.

Main changes, and why

  • The board is a calendar, not a set of category boxes. Categories still drive the recall, but each pin lands on the month it is due. The family then sees when the store empties, not just that the total is too big. Running out is a timing problem, so timing should be what they see.
  • Guess first, then count. The family guesses the month the bags run out, before any recall. After the recall, the app plays the year and shows the gap between the guess and the count. It makes the study's "budget neglect" visible to the family, and people enjoy finding out whether they guessed right.
  • Play the year. The granary empties one moon at a time. The hungry months light up. This is the scene people remember and tell their neighbours about.
  • Khoswe the rat for forgotten costs. Emergencies become a "keep-safe bag" you guard against the rat. It is a friendly way to plan for costs nobody can put a date on. The name and the character need local testing.
  • Take home checkpoints, not a spreadsheet. The result is three dates and a bag count for each ("at Christmas, still have 7 bags"). You can check that against the store by looking. People stopped using the take-home supplies in year two, and a rule of thumb is more likely to get used than a budget.
  • Private by design. A hide button is always one tap away. There is no account, and nothing is uploaded. There is an Erase button. The plan can be done behind a screen in a group session.
  • Next harvest: "What surprised you?" The second-year version opens on last year's checkpoints and asks what was missing. That is a five-minute refresher, not the full hour.

Risks to design around

  • Planning can't make grain. If the harvest is too small, the honest result is "short by N bags". The app must not shame the family or suggest the plan failed. That screen should point to real help (VSLA, safety-net contacts) that the deployer sets up.
  • Shame and gender. Who holds the phone decides whose costs get remembered. Test a mode where two members of the household plan together.
  • Adding information by accident. Default amounts, example families and "most people spend…" hints all change the treatment. In a research arm, the example family below must only show how the screens work, never real amounts.
  • Humour doesn't travel. The rat, the guide and the month symbols are placeholders to test locally. Nyanja/Bemba names still need checking.

Suggested first test

Twenty households, one-on-one, split between the facilitated pin exercise and this app with a facilitator beside them. Measure: what they understand at each screen (ask them to explain it back), how many expenses they remember compared with the pin version, time taken, whether anyone could see their numbers, and whether they can still state their checkpoint two weeks later. Then check grain in store at the first checkpoint.
